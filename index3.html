01 / INTRODUCTION

Why Does This Issue Matter?

Even though international law protects the territorial integrity of states, some territories have been governed separately from a recognized central government for decades.

01 · A legal paradox

The right of peoples to self-determination comes into tension with the principles of territorial integrity and the inviolability of borders.

02 · Political reality

De facto authorities, armed forces, police, and administrative institutions may operate without broad international recognition.

03 · International security

Such territories affect regional conflicts, relations between major powers, migration, and humanitarian conditions.

04 · No universal solution

Negotiations, reintegration, recognition, or a prolonged status quo are different possible outcomes of this type of conflict.

The central paradox: a territory’s de facto autonomy does not automatically mean that its independence will be recognized.
Speaker notes

The issue is relevant because legal norms and political realities do not always coincide. The population of a territory may seek independence, while the central government considers it part of its state. The international community generally seeks to discourage unilateral changes to borders, while also taking self-determination and the rights of the population into account. As a result, such conflicts can persist for decades.

02 / THEORY

What Is an Unrecognized State?

A political entity that claims independence and exercises a significant degree of de facto autonomy, but has not received broad international recognition.

De facto

A territory and population, functioning governing institutions, and control over a substantial part of the territory.

De jure

Recognition of independence by other states and the ability to participate fully in international relations.

Criteria for Statehood — 1933 Montevideo Convention

01 · Population

A permanent population.

02 · Territory

A defined territory.

03 · Government

An organized government.

04 · External relations

The capacity to enter into relations with other states. Having an army is not, in itself, a separate mandatory criterion for statehood.

Speaker notes

Article 1 of the Montevideo Convention lists four classic criteria for statehood: a permanent population, a defined territory, a government, and the capacity to enter into relations with other states. Article 3 states that the political existence of a state is independent of recognition by other states. The UN does not universally “recognize” states: recognition is granted by individual states, while UN membership is a separate process.

03 / INTERNATIONAL LAW

Two Theories of Recognition

Declaratory Theory

A state exists if it meets the objective criteria for statehood. Recognition by other countries confirms an existing fact rather than creating it.

Recognition confirms an existing status.

Constitutive Theory

International recognition plays a key role in acquiring full international legal personality and participating in international relations.

Recognition is a condition for full international status.

Practical implication

Contemporary practice combines elements of both theories: de facto statehood may exist without recognition, but the entity’s diplomatic, economic, and legal opportunities remain limited.

Speaker notes

The declaratory theory is based on objective indicators of statehood and Article 3 of the Montevideo Convention. The constitutive theory emphasizes that participation in the international system is difficult without recognition. In practice, neither approach exists in a pure form. Taiwan has a developed system of self-government, but its diplomatic recognition is limited. Transnistria has its own governing institutions but is not recognized by any UN member state.

04 / TYPOLOGY

Classification Criteria

By degree of recognition

  • Partially recognized: Kosovo, Abkhazia, South Ossetia.
  • Limited diplomatic recognition: Taiwan.
  • Not recognized by UN member states: Transnistria.

Palestine has non-member observer State status at the UN and broad international recognition; its position should be considered separately.

By origin

  • The dissolution of empires and federations.
  • Civil wars and separatist movements.
  • Ethnic and territorial conflicts.
  • The consequences of external intervention.
“Unrecognized state” is an analytical label, not a single official legal category.
Speaker notes

Classification depends on what is being measured: the number of states granting recognition, effective territorial control, the degree of external support, or participation in international institutions. Taiwan and Palestine differ from classic unrecognized entities because of their distinct histories and international positions. It is also important not to conflate a political entity, a partially recognized state, and a territory with autonomous self-government.

05 / GEOGRAPHY

Geography of Contemporary De Facto Entities

Post-Soviet Space

Transnistria — a de facto separate administration since the 1990s.
Abkhazia and South Ossetia — partially recognized; most states consider them part of Georgia.

Europe

Kosovo — declared independence in 2008 and is recognized by a number of states.
Northern Cyprus — recognized only by Türkiye.

Asia

Taiwan — self-government, limited formal diplomatic recognition, and extensive international economic ties.

Africa

Somaliland — declared independence in 1991; Israel recognized it in December 2025, while most states continue to support Somalia’s territorial integrity.

The status of these territories varies; their inclusion here does not mean that they share the same international legal status.

Speaker notes

The post-Soviet cases are connected with the dissolution of the USSR and the armed conflicts of the early 1990s. Kosovo emerged in the context of the breakup of Yugoslavia and the dispute over the territory’s status. Northern Cyprus is linked to the island’s division and conflict between its communities. Taiwan has a distinct history dating from 1949 and exercises substantial de facto autonomy. Somaliland re-established separate governance after the collapse of central authority in Somalia. Its international position changed after Israel recognized it in December 2025.

06 / INTERNAL RESILIENCE

How Do They Survive?

01 · Economy

  • Tax system and domestic market.
  • Trade through neighboring countries and intermediaries.
  • A local or adopted currency.
  • External assistance and remittances.

02 · Politics

  • Legislative and executive institutions.
  • Courts and administrative bodies.
  • Elections and local political institutions.
  • Security forces.

03 · Society

  • Regional and political identity.
  • Distinct symbols and educational institutions.
  • Cultural policy and collective memory.
  • Generational change.
Over time, a temporary political arrangement can become an established social reality.
Speaker notes

Economic survival depends on a combination of domestic regulation and external connections. Transnistria, for example, uses its own currency, but its circulation is limited. Trade often takes place through recognized jurisdictions. Political institutions help the administration manage everyday life and maintain internal legitimacy. Social identity is shaped by education, language, symbols, memories of conflict, and the experience of successive generations. However, domestic institutions do not eliminate dependence on external support.

07 / FOREIGN POLICY

External Factors: The Patron State

A patron state is an external actor that provides political, economic, diplomatic, or military support to a de facto entity.

Forms of support

  • Military and security assistance.
  • Financial assistance and energy resources.
  • Diplomatic advocacy.
  • Institutional and infrastructure assistance.

Examples

  • Türkiye — Northern Cyprus: key political, economic, and military support.
  • Russia — Abkhazia and South Ossetia: recognition and comprehensive support.
  • Russia — Transnistria: support without formal recognition of independence.

Diplomatic alternatives: the case of Taiwan

Trade and cultural offices, unofficial diplomatic channels, and scientific and technological agreements help maintain international contacts without formal diplomatic relations.

External support increases resilience, but may also constrain foreign-policy autonomy.
Speaker notes

A patron state helps compensate for a lack of international resources and security guarantees. However, the patron’s interests do not always coincide with those of the local population and leadership. Support can give a political entity room for maneuver while also creating dependence. Support must be distinguished from legal recognition: Transnistria illustrates that a state can provide assistance without recognizing the territory’s independence.

08 / HUMANITARIAN DIMENSION

Challenges Facing the Population

Legal constraints

  • Passports may not be recognized abroad.
  • Difficulties obtaining visas and crossing borders.
  • Recognition of diplomas and civil-status documents.
  • Company registration and property rights.

Economic difficulties

  • Limited access to banking services.
  • Difficulties attracting investment and obtaining loans.
  • Additional costs for international transactions.
  • Dependence on neighboring economies.

Humanitarian risks

  • Limited access to some international programmes.
  • Risks of human rights violations.
  • Difficulties accessing healthcare and freedom of movement.
  • Vulnerability during armed conflicts.

Important qualification

Lack of recognition does not remove human rights or the applicability of relevant rules of international humanitarian law. Assistance may be delivered through international organizations and recognized states.

Speaker notes

The everyday consequences of this status are particularly visible for ordinary residents. Documents issued by de facto authorities may not be accepted by other states, so people often use documents issued by the recognized state that the international community considers responsible for the territory. Economic restrictions make banking and investment more difficult. International organizations are not necessarily absent: humanitarian programmes may operate through recognized states, special mechanisms, and non-governmental organizations. Lack of recognition does not deprive the population of its basic rights.

09 / HISTORICAL OUTCOMES

How Do Unrecognized Entities’ Stories End?

01 · Reintegration or loss of control

Biafra — defeat in the civil war, 1970.
Chechen Republic of Ichkeria — loss of de facto independence.
Nagorno-Karabakh — came under Azerbaijan’s control in 2023.

02 · International recognition

Eritrea — independence in 1993.
East Timor — independence in 2002.
South Sudan — independence in 2011.

03 · Prolonged status quo

Transnistria — unresolved status since the 1990s.
Northern Cyprus — limited recognition.
Abkhazia and South Ossetia — status remains disputed.

There is no universal outcome: the result depends on the balance of power, negotiations, external support, and the international context.
Speaker notes

The first scenario is the defeat of a separatist project and the restoration of central-government control. The second is a negotiated or internationally supported path to independence. Eritrea, East Timor, and South Sudan became UN members after gaining independence. The third scenario is a prolonged unresolved status: the parties fail to reach an agreement while de facto institutions continue to operate. Following Azerbaijan’s operation in September 2023, Nagorno-Karabakh came under its control, and most of the Armenian population left for Armenia.

10 / CONCLUSION

Conclusion

Unrecognized states are not merely exceptions to the international system; they are a persistent manifestation of the gap between political reality and legal status.

De facto authority ≠ recognition

Having a territory, population, and institutions does not guarantee international legal recognition.

Law and politics are interconnected

Decisions on recognition depend not only on legal arguments but also on states’ interests.

Patronage has a dual effect

External support helps an entity survive, but may create dependence.

The population is the central humanitarian concern

Even where status is disputed, rights, security, and access to assistance must be protected.

The international community must find ways to engage without first resolving the question of status, thereby reducing humanitarian risks.

Thank you for your attention

Speaker notes

Unrecognized states show that the international system cannot be reduced to formal legal categories. Their existence is connected with the history of conflicts, self-determination, territorial integrity, and the interests of external powers. Resolving these situations requires negotiations, mediation, and practical cooperation on humanitarian and social issues. Even when political status remains unresolved, the population must not be deprived of basic rights and protection.
